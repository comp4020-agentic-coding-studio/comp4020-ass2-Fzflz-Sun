The Alignment Lab is the course's central exercise: a small group is given a
real-shaped decision to make together, each person holding one of four roles
and a private brief the others cannot see in full. It is marked as
[The Alignment Lab](/assessments/alignment-lab/) assessment, worth 45% of the
course.

The scenario used for the graded Lab is confidential and distributed by the
teaching team before your group's session — publishing it here would let
later groups prepare against roles they haven't been assigned yet, which
defeats the exercise. This page instead runs you through the process using a
small, fully public worked example, so you can see exactly what a role card
and a decision record look like before you are handed the real ones.

## The four roles

Every Lab scenario is built around the same four functional archetypes, even
though the fictional person and organisation change each time:

- **Executive Sponsor** — owns the outcome the organisation wants, and
  answers for it upward.
- **Delivery Lead** — has to make whatever gets decided actually work.
- **Finance and Risk Lead** — controls whether the decision can be funded,
  and what it might cost if it goes wrong.
- **Staff Representative** — speaks for the people the decision is done to,
  not just the people making it.

Each role's private brief carries the same six things: a public
responsibility everyone can see, one piece of evidence only that role holds,
one constraint they cannot trade away, one form of leverage or dependency
over the others, the minimum outcome they can live with, and the specific
condition under which they would change their position.

## Ground rules, before anyone is assigned a role

These apply to every run of the Lab, and are agreed before role cards are
distributed — not introduced partway through, and not something you discover
only in Week 11's material on the ethics of alignment.

- **No real disputes.** Every scenario is fictional. You will never be asked
  to re-litigate an actual disagreement from your workplace, household or
  student group.
- **Opt-out without penalty.** If a role or scenario would put you in a
  position you are not comfortable with, you can request a swap or an
  alternative individual task before the session, no explanation required.
- **In-role, not in-character.** You are arguing a position, not performing a
  persona. Personal attacks, real names, and anything outside the brief are
  out of bounds, and the session facilitator can pause the room if the line
  is crossed.
- **Nothing said in-role is evidence of what you personally believe.** The
  decision autopsy afterwards is marked on your analysis, not on the position
  your role required you to argue.
- **The record is the record.** Whatever the group's decision record says is
  what gets marked as the group's outcome, even if you privately disagreed —
  which is precisely the situation the decision autopsy asks you to examine.
- **Absence doesn't erase information.** Every role carries a short
  continuity memo alongside its brief, kept up to date through the
  pre-meeting window, so that if the person holding a role is unexpectedly
  absent, the group still has what they knew.
- **Make-up and async participation are always available.** If you miss the
  live meeting, you complete an equivalent asynchronous version rather than
  losing the mark — and no one's grade depends on a groupmate showing up.
- **Peer feedback checks contributions, not popularity.** Any peer input into
  marking asks what a specific person actually contributed — a piece of
  evidence surfaced, a commitment logged, a position changed — never a
  general vote on who did well.
- **You exit the role afterwards.** Once the reveal has happened, the group
  explicitly closes the exercise before moving on: what a role did in the
  simulation is discussed as the role's behaviour, not as a judgement of the
  classmate who played it.

## How a run of the Lab works

0. **Rehearsal.** A small, ungraded run of this whole process — using the
   public worked example below — before anyone sits the assessed Lab.
1. **Public materials and initial judgment.** Before any role is assigned,
   everyone reads the same public materials and submits an individual
   initial judgment: what they think should happen, on the record, before
   anyone has a stake in a role.
2. **Role assignment and strategy memo.** Each person receives one
   confidential role brief and, individually, submits a one-page strategy
   memo: their responsibilities, their evidence, what they don't know, who
   they plan to contact, and the ethical boundaries they won't cross.
3. **Pre-meeting contact window** (48–72 hours). By default, each person may
   make at most **two self-initiated contacts** — but can reply to as many
   requests from others as they like. Every contact, sent or received, is
   logged by both parties: its purpose, any new information, any commitments
   made, and whether either person's judgment changed. The simulator below
   shows the shape of this window using the public worked example.
4. **The meeting.** Live or synchronous, one sitting of around 25 minutes, no
   extensions on the clock once it starts.
5. **Decision record.** The group produces one record — the decision or a
   reasoned deferral, who holds final authority over it, evidence versus
   unverified assumption, unresolved dissent, who owns the resulting actions,
   and what would reopen it — using the
   [decision record template](/templates/). Consensus is not required.
6. **Reveal and consequence test.** Every role brief is shared with the whole
   group, then a choice-based consequence test runs against the decision
   record. Its outcomes come from explicit, traceable case rules, not from
   chance or dramatic licence — it is a teaching model of one plausible
   consequence, not a claim that a real decision like it would play out the
   same way.
7. **Individual autopsy and partial rerun.** Individually: a decision
   autopsy naming what you now think actually decided the outcome, then a
   short rerun of one specific juncture to test whether a named process
   change would have produced a different one.

## Worked example: should the Reading Room move to hot-desking?

This is a small, entirely public scenario used only to demonstrate the
mechanics above. It is not the graded Lab scenario.

Facilities wants to convert 40 assigned desks in the Reading Room into
unassigned hot-desks, to fit a larger cohort. Staff were not consulted before
the proposal was drafted. Four people, one for each archetype, have to reach
a decision.

### The four role cards, fully revealed

Because this is the public example, every field of every role card is shown
below — which is exactly what would **not** happen before a real, graded run
of the Lab.

| Archetype | Public responsibility | Minimum acceptable outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Executive Sponsor | Owns the space-efficiency target the Vice-Chancellery set this year. | Any plan that raises reported utilisation above 50% within two terms. |
| Delivery Lead | Has to implement whatever the room's new layout turns out to be. | A decision that does not commit to a go-live date before the software question is answered. |
| Finance and Risk Lead | Signs off any spend the room change requires. | A named, costed proposal, even a small one, rather than an in-principle agreement to spend later. |
| Staff Representative | Represents the 12 staff who have used the same desk for years. | Named, reserved desks for anyone with a documented accessibility need. |

Each role also holds one piece of evidence only they have — the utilisation
report, the booking-system export, the cost comparison, the accessibility
equipment list — which is why the pre-meeting contact window above matters:
whoever spends their 72 hours well walks into the meeting knowing things the
others do not.

## What you submit

See [The Alignment Lab](/assessments/alignment-lab/) for the full marking
breakdown. In short: an initial judgment, a strategy memo, a negotiation log,
one shared decision record, an individual decision autopsy, and a partial
rerun of one key juncture.
