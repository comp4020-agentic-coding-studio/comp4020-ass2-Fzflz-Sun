## Before the session

This course's central skill is reading a paper trail without letting it read
you first. Before you can ask whether a decision was pre-made, you need to be
able to tell, entry by entry, what a document actually proves versus what you
are filling in yourself.

Three kinds of claim, used all semester:

- **Fact** — a dated document states this directly, and you can name which
  one.
- **Inference** — a reasonable reading of the documents, but one more step
  removed from what any single document states outright. Someone could
  disagree with it without being unreasonable.
- **Unknown** — the public record does not settle this either way. Saying so
  honestly is not a failure to find the answer; it is itself a finding.

Read the case file below — ten dated entries from the Engagement Signal
Pilot's first six weeks, Slop University's fictional staff engagement-scoring
rollout, which the whole course follows. For each entry, write down which of
the three kinds you think it is, and one sentence why.

### Case file: the Engagement Signal Pilot, January–March

| Date | Entry | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2021-01-18 | Finance & Risk signs off the ClarityPulse budget line | Finance & Risk sign-off memo, dated and initialled |
| 2021-01-22 | Tomas Herrera and ClarityPulse's sales lead meet | Calendar entry, both attendees named |
| 2021-01-29 | An implementation-timeline draft names LSS as the first rollout site | Draft project plan, version history shows one editor |
| 2021-02-01 to 2021-02-28 | Dr Femi Adisa's staff first hear the word "ClarityPulse" at all | No dated document confirms this |
| 2021-02-10 | Priya Nandakumar emails the Vice-Chancellery an update naming a go-live term | Email, forwarded and time-stamped |
| 2021-02-24 | Sanjay Okoro requests a staff information session before rollout | Email from the Staff Association distribution list |
| 2021-03-01 | Wren Castellano drafts the Steering Committee's Item 4 as an open question | Draft agenda file, timestamped, never circulated |
| 2021-03-03 | Wren Castellano sends Item 4 as a confirmation instead | Sent agenda, distribution list and read receipts |
| 2021-03-01 to 2021-03-03 | Between the two drafts, someone decided the item should read as settled | No document names who, or why — this entry has no date of its own, only the gap between the two drafts around it |
| 2021-03-08 | The Steering Committee meets and confirms the timeline | Meeting minutes, attendance list attached |

This is deliberately not a real institution's leaked records — it is teaching
fiction, invented for this course and reused across the semester so the case
can build rather than restart each week.

## In the session

In pairs: compare your ten classifications before either of you sees the
answer key. Where you disagree, that disagreement is the exercise — argue it
from the document, not from what seems plausible. Then build a single shared
timeline document from all ten entries, in order, each with its agreed kind
and a one-line justification. Keep this; you will use the same format for a
real decision in the [Decision Autopsy](/assessments/decision-autopsy/)
assessment in Week 6, and the [decision-autopsy template](/templates/) uses
this exact structure.

### What good analysis of one entry looks like

Take the "between the two drafts, someone decided the item should read as
settled" entry, dated only to the 1–3 March gap between the drafts either
side of it:

> **Kind: inference, not fact.** No document names who made this call or
> states a reason. The inference is supported by the two drafts existing at
> all — something changed the item's framing between 1 and 3 March. But a
> different, equally reasonable inference is available: the underlying
> question may have been genuinely settled in an earlier, undocumented
> conversation, and Draft A simply reflects that a decision had already been
> reasonably reached, not that it was concealed. The public record supports
> "something changed" far more strongly than it supports "why it changed" —
> and does not by itself support either "the process worked as it should" or
> "the process pre-empted the room."

That last sentence is the standard the rest of the course holds you to: naming
what the evidence does and does not support, including the possibility that it
supports no strong conclusion at all.

## A little further reading

Any research-methods or library "evaluating sources" guide covers the
primary/secondary source distinction this exercise builds on — your own
university library almost certainly has one online. Read the part that
distinguishes a primary document from a claim inferred from it, and bring one
question to class: where does an *inference drawn from several primary
documents together* sit, if it isn't quite either?
