## Before the session

Read both drafts of the Steering Committee agenda for the Engagement Signal
Pilot, below. They describe the same meeting, on the same day, with the same
attendees. Come with an answer to one question: which version makes it easier
to vote no, and why?

## In the session

An agenda does three quiet arguments before anyone speaks: it names what is
"on the table" (and by omission, what is not), it orders items so that early
decisions constrain later ones, and it allocates time in a way that signals
how much disagreement is expected. None of this requires bad faith — a
competent chair does all three routinely, often without noticing. The skill
this week teaches is noticing on purpose, in both directions: reading someone
else's agenda for what it argues, and writing your own honestly.

### The case: two agendas, one meeting

Wren Castellano, Slop University's Internal Communications Lead, drafted two
versions of the agenda for the Steering Committee meeting that will discuss
the Engagement Signal Pilot — the same pilot the semester's
case follows. Both are real drafts that circulated; only one was sent.

**Draft A — sent to the distribution list on 3 March:**

> **Steering Committee — Engagement Signal Pilot**
> **Item 4: Confirming the ClarityPulse implementation timeline**
> _Purpose: note the agreed go-live date and assign rollout tasks._
> _Time allocated: 10 minutes_

**Draft B — found in Wren's shared drive, dated 1 March, never sent:**

> **Steering Committee — Engagement Signal Pilot**
> **Item 4: Whether to proceed with the ClarityPulse pilot**
> _Purpose: decide whether the pilot proceeds, is delayed, or is redesigned
> before it reaches the Library & Student Services floor._
> _Time allocated: 35 minutes_

Draft A's title assumes the answer; its purpose statement gives the room
nothing to do but logistics; its ten minutes signals that disagreement, if any
surfaces, was not planned for. Draft B's title states an open question; its
purpose statement includes the option to say no; its thirty-five minutes
budgets for an actual discussion. The decision about which draft to send was
never itself put on an agenda — which is, in this course's terms, exactly
where you would expect to find it.

## Exercise

Take an agenda item from your own work, study group, share house or club —
something real, however small. Write it twice: once so it reads as already
decided, once so it reads as genuinely open. Bring both. In pairs, trade
agendas with someone else and guess, from the wording alone, which one you
were handed.
