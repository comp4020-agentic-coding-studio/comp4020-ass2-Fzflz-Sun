## The brief

> Take a decision-making process — the semester's case, your Alignment Lab
> group, or a real process you have access to — and redesign it so that its
> next decision is more challengeable, more traceable, and clearer about who
> is accountable for it.

This is the course's thesis turned into a design problem, and the goal is not
to eliminate pre-meeting coordination. Weeks 1–10 argued that talking before a
formal meeting is often how a decision gets made *well* — by working through
disagreement somewhere a fixed agenda has no room for it — and just as often
how it gets made badly, by settling something before the people it affects
ever get a documented chance to weigh in. A redesign that simply bans
conversation before the meeting does not fix that; it just pushes the same
trade-off somewhere less visible. The Alignment Lab gave you first-hand
evidence of how your own group's process actually worked, against how it
looked on paper. Use that evidence to design a process where, whatever
coordination happens before the room, the eventual decision can still be
questioned, traced back to what informed it, and pinned to someone who
answers for it — not a process with no informal channel at all.

## What you submit

A redesign document, 1,800–2,400 words, that:

1. Names the process being redesigned and who currently makes its decisions.
2. States what is wrong with it today, citing specific evidence — from the
   semester's case, from your own Alignment Lab audit, or both. "Wrong" here
   means hard to challenge, trace, or attribute — not "involves informal
   conversation before the meeting."
3. Proposes a concrete replacement: who is invited, when, on what information,
   with what record kept and by whom, and what channel (if any) still exists
   for the kind of informal, pre-meeting coordination that Weeks 1–10 showed
   can be genuinely useful.
4. Names who loses influence under the new design, and does not pretend the
   redesign is free. Slower, more accountable decisions cost someone
   something — usually the people who benefited from speed or from
   low visibility.
5. Tests the replacement against at least one concrete scenario — a specific
   decision the current process would have to face — and walks through what
   the new design would actually produce, step by step.

A redesign that makes everyone equally powerful is not a redesign; it is a
wish, and neither is a redesign that pretends no one will ever talk before the
meeting again. The strongest submissions make a specific, defensible
trade-off between speed, coordination and accountability, and argue for it.

Teaching sessions end with Week 12; this submission is due two weeks later,
giving you time away from the weekly schedule to write it up properly rather
than rushing it out on the last day of class.
