## The brief

> Take a real decision you were not part of, and read its paper trail the way
> Weeks 1–5 taught you to: as evidence, not as a story someone already told
> you.

Pick a decision with a documented trail: minutes, an agenda, an announcement,
a press release, a public consultation record. A council rezoning, a
university policy change, a company's product shutdown, a sporting body's
ruling — anything with dates you can put in order and documents you can link
to. The point is not to guess at motives you cannot evidence. It is to show,
from the record itself, what it does and does not support — about where the
real decision happened relative to the meeting that announced it, and about
how confident the record itself lets you be.

A strong autopsy does not need to find a seam. If your timeline shows a
process that was genuinely open until the recorded meeting, or shows evidence
that is currently indeterminate either way, say so and argue it — that is not
a weaker answer than finding a pre-decided point, provided you show the same
rigour: the alternative explanations you considered, and why the record
supports the conclusion you reached rather than another one.

## What you submit

A written autopsy, 1,200–1,600 words, structured as:

1. **The decision**, stated as a single sentence, with a link to where it was
   announced.
2. **The timeline**, every document you can date, in order, each with a
   one-line note on what it shows.
3. **The seam, or why there isn't one** — one of three conclusions, each
   acceptable if the argument is evidenced:
   - **A seam**: the specific point in the timeline where the outcome looks
     settled before the forum that supposedly settled it, the evidence that
     supports it, and at least one alternative explanation you considered
     and could rule out, with the reason why.
   - **A genuinely open process**: a reasoned case, from the timeline, that
     no such point exists.
   - **Indeterminate**: a reasoned case that the record cannot currently
     decide between a seam and an open process — naming the specific
     evidence that is missing (the thing that, if it existed, would settle
     it), a specific alternative explanation you could not rule out with
     what the record actually contains, and what would change your
     judgement if that missing evidence turned up. Being unable to rule an
     alternative out is what makes a conclusion indeterminate; ruling one
     out belongs to the first conclusion above, not this one.
4. **What you cannot show**, the gap between what the documents prove and
   what you suspect. Naming this gap honestly is part of the mark.

Cite every document you use. A claim with no citation does not count as
evidence, even if it is true.
