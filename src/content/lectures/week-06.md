## A record of what was said is also a record of what wasn't

Minutes are written as if they are a transparent window onto a meeting —
whoever wasn't in the room reads them to find out what happened. They are
never that. Every set of minutes is a compression: someone decided what
counted as "discussion" worth recording, what counted as noise, and what
counted as nothing at all. This week's document is the minutes of the same
13 March LSS Rollout Working Group meeting that Week 4 introduced through a
calendar invite and an agenda. Read them, and a gap becomes visible that
neither of those two documents could show on its own.

## What the minutes do, and don't, record

Sanjay Okoro's question is in the minutes — that much the record does show.
What the record does not show is any answer to it. "The sequence was
confirmed as presented" sits directly after the question with nothing
between them: no response, no discussion, no vote noted, no dissent
recorded either for or against. Read narrowly, that is simply how minutes
work — they record outcomes, not every exchange. Read against Week 4's
agenda, which allocated five minutes to this item and described the
sequence as something to "confirm" rather than "discuss," the silence
starts to look like the agenda's plan playing out exactly as scheduled: a
question was asked, no space had been allocated for it to be answered, and
the item closed on time.

This is the week's central distinction. A minuted silence is not proof that
a concern was ignored — the minute-taker may simply not have recorded a
verbal response that did happen, which is a failure of note-taking, not of
process. It is also not proof that nothing happened — a chair moving
straight from a question to a confirmation, in a room, in real time, is a
choice, whether or not anyone in the room experienced it as one. The
minutes alone cannot tell you which. What they can tell you is that the gap
exists, and that whoever wrote the agenda controlled how much room there
was for it to be filled.

## Why this belongs next to the Decision Autopsy

The Decision Autopsy asks you to make exactly this kind of call on a real
decision: to look at what a record does and doesn't show, name the gap
honestly, and argue a specific, evidenced conclusion rather than retreating
into "the record is ambiguous" as a way of avoiding the work. A rigorously
argued "the record is genuinely indeterminate, and here is why, and here is
the alternative explanation I considered and ruled out" is one of the
assessment's acceptable conclusions, not a weaker one — provided it does the
same work as any other reading: alternatives considered, and a specific
reason the record supports indeterminacy rather than a pre-decided or a
genuinely open process. What doesn't meet the mark is the undeveloped
version of the same word: "it's ambiguous, so I can't say," offered with no
alternative considered and no case made for why the record actually is
unresolvable.
