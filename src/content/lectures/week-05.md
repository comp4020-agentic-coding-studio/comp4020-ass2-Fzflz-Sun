## Two very different reasons a document arrives late

A document that arrives close to a decision can mean two entirely different
things, and the record rarely announces which. It can mean someone is
managing what the room has time to react to. It can also mean someone found
something late, said so as soon as they had it in writing, and the timing is
just the honest shape of when the analysis finished. Week 4 taught you to
read a stack of documents for a pattern; this week asks a narrower question
of a single document — not what it says, but what its own arrival date does
to the people who receive it.

## The story continues: a risk addendum, sent the night before

Beatrix Oyelaran, the pilot's Finance and Risk Lead, sends the Working
Group a memo on 21 March — one day before a scheduled sign-off meeting for
Phase 2 spend — flagging that ClarityPulse's default configuration retains
individual-level engagement scores for 24 months, not the 6 months the
original proposal referenced. The full memo is reproduced on the [session
page](/sessions/05-information-arrives-on-someones-schedule/); read it
there before continuing.

## Reading the timing, not just the content

The content of this memo is not in dispute — a genuine retention
discrepancy, worth flagging, is exactly the kind of thing a Finance and Risk
Lead should raise. The question this week teaches you to ask separately is
what the *timing* does: a Working Group that receives a memo like this the
night before a sign-off meeting has, in practice, two options — delay the
sign-off to actually resolve the discrepancy, or approve on schedule with
the risk merely noted rather than addressed. Both outcomes are available in
the record either way; what changes is which one is *easy*. A recommendation
delivered the day before is procedurally present and practically foreclosed
at the same time — the memo carries no timestamp finer than its date, so
the honest claim is "the day before," not a specific number of hours
nobody wrote down — and that gap between what a document permits
and what it makes convenient is exactly the "stacking" pattern from Week 4
applied to a single artefact instead of several.

## The honest counter-case, stated plainly

The memo says the confirming answer "received today" — if that is true, the
timing may simply be the honest shape of when Finance actually learned the
fact, not a choice about when to reveal it. The memo itself is dated and sent
21 March, a Sunday, one day ahead of the 22 March Working Group meeting: a
vendor's written confirmation landing on a Sunday and going straight to the
Working Group the same day it arrived is not evidence of manipulation by
itself; it is what diligence sometimes looks like. What the
record does not show, and what you cannot get from this memo alone, is *when
Beatrix first asked ClarityPulse the question* — and that missing date is
precisely the kind of gap the Decision Autopsy will ask you to name honestly
as unresolved, rather than resolving it by assumption in either direction.

## A little further reading

The linked paper is not about meetings or memos at all — it's a finance
study of when companies choose to release earnings news. Its finding: firms
disproportionately release worse-than-expected earnings on Fridays, when
investor attention is measurably lower, and the market's immediate reaction
to Friday announcements is smaller while the delayed reaction, once the
news gets noticed, is larger. Read it for one purpose: the mechanism, not
the venue — real, measured evidence that *when* unwelcome information is
released changes how much immediate scrutiny it gets, independent of what
the information says. That is exactly as far as the analogy to Beatrix's
memo goes: this is aggregate evidence across many companies' disclosure
choices, not proof of what any one sender intended by any one memo on any
one Sunday. The paper can tell you the pattern exists at scale; it cannot
tell you whether this memo is an instance of it, and neither can you —
which is precisely the kind of claim the Decision Autopsy will ask you to
leave as unknown rather than resolve by analogy.
