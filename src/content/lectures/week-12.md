## What the semester actually built up

Eleven weeks have looked at one long-running fictional case from a series
of separate angles: who was invited and on what terms (Week 2), how an
agenda argues before anyone speaks (Week 3), what forms before the formal
meeting does (Week 4), what timing does to a document regardless of its
content (Week 5), what a minuted silence does and doesn't prove (Week 6),
how a room sorts contributions by whether they help or complicate (Week 7),
and what a dissent needs to survive being written down (Week 8). None of
these were separate flaws to fix one at a time. They are seven views of the
same underlying object: a decision process, and the specific points in it
where accountability can quietly leak out.

Redesigning a process means naming that object directly rather than
patching the view you happened to look through last. A redesign that fixes
the invite list but leaves the agenda's naming and timing untouched has not
actually made the process more accountable — it has moved the leak
somewhere else in the same pipe.

## Three questions any redesign has to answer

- **Who is invited, and on what right?** Not everyone affected needs a
  vote — Week 2 already showed that "advise" and "vote" can both be
  legitimate, depending on the seat. A redesign has to state the right
  explicitly, rather than leaving it implied by who happens to show up.
- **What record survives, and who controls what goes in it?** Weeks 6
  through 8 showed how much a decision's memory depends on who writes it
  down and what they choose to compress. A redesign names who takes the
  record, what it must include (not just outcomes — unanswered questions,
  dissent, and who raised it), and who can contest it afterwards.
- **What informal channel still exists, and is it visible?** Week 4's
  "meeting before the meeting" is not automatically a problem — informal
  coordination before a formal meeting is often exactly how disagreement
  gets worked through in a room too small or too public to do it live.
  The redesign question is not whether an informal channel exists, but
  whether its existence and its limits are visible to the people it
  affects, rather than something they have to infer from a pattern of
  documents the way you have spent this semester learning to.

## Bringing your own Alignment Lab audit into it

Your Decision System Redesign, due two weeks from today, is explicitly
asked to use evidence from your own Alignment Lab decision autopsy — not
just the pilot case. You now have two data sets: eleven weeks of someone
else's decision, read from the outside, and one decision you were inside
of yourself, with a role card, real leverage, and a record you helped
write. The strongest redesigns in past years have used the second data set
to catch something the first one alone could not show — what a process
actually feels like to operate inside of, not just to read afterwards.
