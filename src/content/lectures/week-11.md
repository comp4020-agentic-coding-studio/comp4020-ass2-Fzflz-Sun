## Two fairness questions, not one

"Was that fair?" usually means two different questions wearing one word.
**Procedural fairness** asks whether everyone with a stake got a genuine
chance to be heard, by a process applied consistently. **Distributive
fairness** asks whether the outcome itself distributes benefit and burden
in a way that can be defended. A decision can pass one test and fail the
other in either direction. The 13 March Working Group followed its own
agenda, on time, with a question asked and a decision reached — procedurally
unremarkable — while a phase order that puts the highest-traffic team first,
with no consultation on the reasons, may still land unevenly on the people
who work that desk. Conversely, a process that consults everyone at length
can still land on an outcome someone reasonably calls unfair, if the
consultation never had any real chance of changing the result.

The [Alignment Lab's hot-desking worked example](/alignment-lab/) makes the
same split visible on paper: the Staff Representative's `constraint` field
requires taking any agreement back to affected staff before it holds
(a procedural guarantee), while the `minimumAcceptableOutcome` field —
named, reserved desks for documented accessibility needs — is a
distributive claim about what the outcome itself must guarantee, regardless
of how the conversation that reaches it goes. A group could satisfy the
first and still fail the second, or the reverse.

## Leverage asymmetry is not automatically unfair

Week 9 introduced leverage as a fourth axis alongside decision rights. This
week's harder question is when an asymmetry in leverage becomes an ethical
problem rather than just a structural fact. A Finance and Risk Lead's
sign-off requirement over $10,000 is a large, formal piece of leverage that
nobody in the process is likely to call unfair — it exists for a reason
everyone can name, applies consistently regardless of who holds the seat,
and is visible to everyone in the room. An asymmetry becomes harder to
defend when it is invisible to the people it affects, applied
inconsistently, or exists for no defensible reason beyond who happened to
get there first. The test this week teaches is not "is there an asymmetry"
— there almost always is — but whether that asymmetry could survive being
explained, out loud, to the person with less of it.

## Applying this to your own Lab record

Your Alignment Lab decision record and negotiation log, from the assessment
due today, are the material for this test. Somewhere in your own group's
process there was very likely a leverage asymmetry that shaped the outcome.
The question worth sitting with before you write today's individual
autopsy is not whether that asymmetry existed — it did, in some form, in
every group — but whether it could have been said out loud in the room
without anyone objecting to its legitimacy.
