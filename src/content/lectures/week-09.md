## Four kinds of standing, not two

Week 2 sorted the Engagement Signal Pilot's Steering Committee into four
decision rights: **vote**, **advise**, **informed-after**, and
**not-invited**. That sort was about who is even present and what their
seat formally entitles them to. This week adds a second axis on top of it,
because formal standing and practical leverage are not the same thing.
Someone can hold a vote and still have no real ability to block an outcome
they dislike, if nothing they control makes disagreement costly to the
other side. Someone else can hold no vote at all and still shape the
outcome, if they hold something the room needs.

The Alignment Lab's four roles — Executive Sponsor, Delivery Lead, Finance
and Risk Lead, Staff Representative — are built around this same split,
deliberately mirroring the pilot's own cast (a sponsor who owns the go-live
date, a delivery lead who runs the ClarityPulse relationship, a finance and risk
lead who holds sign-off authority, a staff representative with an advisory
seat and no formal vote). The role cards you will receive this week each
carry a `leverageOrDependency` field for exactly this reason: what you can
actually make expensive for someone else to ignore is often a better guide
to your real position than the label on your seat.

## Reading your own role card for leverage, not just position

When you receive your role card this week, resist the instinct to read only
your `publicResponsibility` and `minimumAcceptableOutcome` — the two fields
that describe *what you want*. The `leverageOrDependency` field describes
*what you can do about it*, and it is frequently smaller or larger than the
seat's formal standing would suggest. A Finance and Risk Lead's leverage is
usually large and formal — a sign-off requirement written into policy. A
Staff Representative's leverage is usually informal and slower — the
ability to escalate, to withhold cooperation, to make a decision
politically costly after the fact rather than blocking it before. Neither
kind of leverage is fake. They operate on different timescales, and a
strategy memo that only accounts for the fast, formal kind will
consistently underestimate what a Staff Representative can actually do.
