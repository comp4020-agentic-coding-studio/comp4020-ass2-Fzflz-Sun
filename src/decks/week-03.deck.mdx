# The Agenda Is an Argument

Week 3 — Framing the table

---

## Three quiet arguments

Every agenda makes three arguments before anyone speaks.

- **naming** — what the item is called, and what that implies is already true
- **ordering** — what gets decided first, and what that then constrains
- **timing** — how many minutes signal how much disagreement was expected

---

## Naming

"Confirming the rollout timeline" and "whether to proceed with the rollout"
can describe the same meeting.

Only one of them leaves room for "no" as a legitimate answer.

---

## Ordering

Decide the budget before you decide the goal, and the goal now has to fit the
budget — not the other way round.

The order was itself a choice. It rarely appears as one.

---

## Timing

Ten minutes for an item says: this is logistics.

Thirty-five minutes for the same item says: this might not go the way it
looks like it's going to go.

A chair does not have to say which is true. The clock says it for them.

## The case: two drafts, one item

Slop University's Steering Committee is considering the Engagement Signal
Pilot — a staff engagement-scoring platform, ClarityPulse, proposed for a
first rollout in Library & Student Services.

Two agenda drafts exist for the same meeting, same date, same attendees.

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## Draft A — sent

**Item 4: Confirming the ClarityPulse implementation timeline**

_Purpose: note the agreed go-live date and assign rollout tasks._

_10 minutes_

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## Draft B — never sent

**Item 4: Whether to proceed with the ClarityPulse pilot**

_Purpose: decide whether the pilot proceeds, is delayed, or is redesigned._

_35 minutes_

---

## What changed between drafts

| Aspect | Draft A (sent) | Draft B (unsent) |
|---|---|---|
| Title | assumes an outcome | states an open question |
| Purpose | logistics only | includes "no" and "redesign" |
| Time | 10 minutes | 35 minutes |

The choice between them was never itself on an agenda.

---

## Two readings, not one

**Less charitable:** the outcome was decided, and the agenda rewritten to
match.

**Equally available:** the open question was genuinely resolved between the
two drafts — in a budget call, a vendor conversation — and Draft A reflects
that honestly.

The documents alone cannot tell you which. That gap is real either way.

---

## A working counter-example

Agenda items move from "open" to "confirming" legitimately all the time —
whenever a working group resolves something before it reaches a steering
committee.

The skill is not suspicion of settled-sounding agendas. It is noticing an
undocumented change of status, and being honest about what it does and
doesn't prove.

---

## What a chair controls

- the wording of the item
- its position in the running order
- the minutes allocated to it

## What a chair merely inherits

- who is in the room
- what was already decided upstream, off the record

---

## Exercise debrief

You rewrote a real agenda item twice — once settled, once open — and traded
with a partner.

What gave each version away? Usually one word in the title, and the number of
minutes next to it.
